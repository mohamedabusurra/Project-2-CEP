# Video Script: Seeing Is No Longer Believing

**Course:** CEP146 — Essential Tooling for Programmers
**Topic:** AI safety and ethics — deepfakes, synthetic scams, and the collapse of proof
**Video Length:** 2:30 – 3:00 minutes (~410 words spoken)
**Format:** Three speakers, real voices. No AI-generated narration.

> **Change from our last project:** last time one person carried the narration. This time the script is split into three marked turns so every member is on camera and the handoffs are part of the pacing.

---

## Full Script

---

### INTRODUCTION — *Speaker 1 (JG)* — slides 1–2 — ~0:00–0:40

In February 2024, a finance worker in Hong Kong joined a video call with his CFO.

Everyone on the call looked right. Everyone sounded right.

Not one of them was real.

He authorised fifteen transfers. Twenty-five point six million dollars, gone.

That is not a story about one company being careless.

In a 2025 study, only one in a thousand people could reliably tell real media from AI-generated media.

Today we're talking about deepfakes — and about what happens to a society when a video stops being evidence.

---

### WHAT IT ACTUALLY IS — *Speaker 2 (MC)* — slides 3–4 — ~0:40–1:15

A deepfake isn't magic. It's a copy machine for people.

You take footage that already exists — a podcast, a conference talk, a birthday video on someone's public profile.

A model learns how that face moves and how that voice sounds.

Then it re-renders the person saying things they never said.

Three seconds of audio is enough to clone a voice.

And here's the part that connects to our last project.

We said the cloud removed the barrier between an idea and a global product.

It removed exactly the same barrier for a lie.

The compute that trains a forgery is rented by the minute, by anyone, for the price of a coffee.

---

### WHERE IT LANDS — *Speaker 3 (MA)* — slides 5–6 — ~1:15–1:55

So where does that actually hit?

It hits your phone. Cloned voices turn a scam call into a family emergency.

It hits your feed. After the UK earthquake in December 2025, a fake photo of a collapsed bridge spread fast enough to cancel trains.

And it hits your money. In January 2026, the Bombay Stock Exchange had to warn investors about a deepfake of its own CEO handing out stock tips. It was taken down. By March, it was back.

The FBI logged eight hundred and ninety-three million dollars in AI-related fraud losses in 2025 — the first year it counted them separately.

And most victims never report at all.

---

### WHY WE CAN'T JUST LOOK HARDER — *Speaker 2 (MC)* — slides 7–8 — ~1:55–2:25

Everyone watching this thinks they'd spot it.

Humans detect high-quality fakes at about coin-flip accuracy. Detection tools that score ninety-six percent in a lab lose nearly half of that on real footage.

And the harm isn't only financial. The most common use of this technology has always been fake intimate imagery of real people, made from ordinary photos. Since May 2026, US platforms have forty-eight hours to take that content down once a victim reports it.

---

### CONCLUSION — *Speaker 1 (JG)* — slides 9–10 — ~2:25–2:55

The response is arriving. As of the second of August this year, the EU requires deepfakes to be labelled, with fines up to fifteen million euros.

But the thing that protects you tonight is smaller than a law.

Hang up. Call back on a number you already had. Agree a code word with your family.

That defeats a twenty-five-million-dollar attack, for free.

We're Group 3 — and here's our question.

**If a video can no longer prove anything, what do we accept as proof instead?**

---

## Delivery Notes

- Total target: **2 minutes 30 seconds to 3 minutes**. Read at a natural pace — do not rush the numbers.
- Pause a full beat after "Not one of them was real."
- Say figures the way you'd say them out loud ("eight hundred and ninety-three million"), not as they appear on the slide.
- Slide 8 is read plainly and quickly. No examples, no names, no images.
- Hold on the final question in silence for two seconds before cutting.
